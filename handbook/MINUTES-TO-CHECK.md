# Council minutes to pull, and what each would settle

Built 2026-09-06 from Kenny's CDNC phrase search on **"ordinance changing"**
(handbook/RENAMING-SOURCES.md Step 2b). Twenty-one clippings, 1883–1896, are in
`inbox/`; none has been made into a document yet and **none of the stages below
has been verified against the minutes**. That is what this list is for.

**How to read the Stage column.** §1 = asked (petition), §2 = ordered drafted or
recommended, §3 = adopted, §3+ = adopted AND approved by the mayor, ✔TEXT = the
ordinance's own text is in hand from the Herald. Only §3+ and ✔TEXT are safe to
build a change row from; everything else is a lead, and 1883 alone shows two
cases of the name changing between §2 and §3 (Union Avenue → Pearl, Alabama →
Georgia Bell).

**Meeting dates are inferred** from the paper's date unless the item says
otherwise ("last night", or a certification naming the meeting). Where a
certification names the meeting, that date is exact and marked ✔.

## The November 1889 numbering list — worth its own pass

Published in the Herald of **1889-11-06** and adopted **1889-11-12**, this is the
ordinance that turned the streets south of Pico into numbers. Two of its lines
answer questions this project already had open:

- **"from Pine and Rowland to Sixteenth street"** — the "(formerly Pine St)" of
  the 1893 Miguel Subdivision (documents/mr053-016).
- **"from Laurel and Adele to Fifteenth street"** — very likely the "(formerly
  Laura St)" of the same sheet, if Laura is a misreading of Laurel. Check the
  plat again before believing it.

Also in the list: California/Consuelo/Greenwell/Vejar → Fourteenth; Morris and
Montgomery → Seventeenth; Pacheco and Ocean → Eighteenth; Queen → Twentieth;
York and Locust → Twenty-first; King → Twenty-second; Manhattan → Twenty-third;
Jenkins → Twenty-fourth; Kinney → Twenty-fifth; Rodney/Monroe/Alice →
Twenty-seventh; Montague Ave and Mattie → Twenty-eighth; Shaw → Twenty-ninth;
Brooklyn → Thirtieth; Seville → Thirty-first; Goodman and Dana → Thirty-second;
Aruta → Thirty-third; Shafer → Thirty-fifth; Lanterman and Grace → Thirty-sixth;
Simpson and Edna → Thirty-seventh; Janes/Keller/Sanbran → Thirty-eighth; Ruby
and Ames → Thirty-ninth; Church → Lyon.

**Note the seven-year arc.** Streets renamed to numbers south of Pico in Nov.
1889; the same names appearing again in the Sept. 1896 southwest ordinance
(Shafer, Simpson, Janes); and the 1896 petition asking that names be changed
"so as to make them compare with the names of the streets on the north side of
Pico Street". The 1889, 1896 and 1897 acts are one campaign in three pushes, not
three separate tidyings.

### The answer to the question that prompted this pass

Kenny asked whether all this would tell us *"whether we might find full text of
ordinances or lists of streets on particular dates in either LAH or the council
minutes."* Having read the whole run, the answer is sharper than expected, and
it is **not symmetrical between the two sources**:

**The Herald prints a full list only when the list is the story.** Across 49
clippings 1897–1927 there is not one printed enumeration of a large renaming
ordinance. Ordinance No. 48 (1889) and the Oct. 1888 committee report — both
already in hand — remain the only two full lists this newspaper ever gave us,
and both are from the 1880s, when the Herald still printed legal notices at
length. After about 1890 the paper reports that an ordinance exists, names two
or three streets as examples, and stops. The ninety-street ordinance of 1905
gets four articles across six days and **one** concrete change out of ninety
(`documents/lah-1905-05-13`, "Forty-ninth street … will be changed to
Forty-first street"). The 1908 southwestern ordinance, the 1900 Garvanza
ordinance, the 1919 Sawtelle ordinance and the 1910 harbor ordinance print
**no** names at all beyond illustrative ones.

**So for lists, the minutes are the only route** — and the newspaper's real
value is that it tells us *which meeting* to pull, and, crucially, *whether the
ordinance survived*. That second thing the minutes alone will not tell us
quickly.

**The Herald's comparative advantage is failure.** Six of the largest
instruments in this run were adopted and then killed: the ninety-street
ordinance recalled from the mayor (1905), the Orange-to-Wilshire ordinance
repealed (1908), the Valley → Hughes ordinance held up (1917), the Sherman way
ordinance protested by 74% of owners (1926), plus the two 1897 reversals we
already had. **A minutes-first strategy would have written every one of those
as a change.** Read the newspaper first, then pull the minutes.

### Two documents that are not Los Angeles

`documents/lah-1899-07-26` and `documents/lah-1899-10-11` are **Pasadena**
council news (Fair Oaks, Raymond, Marengo, Orange Grove, St. John's). Their
coverage polygons are a Pasadena box, not the city-limits box, so nothing in
them can be matched against a Los Angeles street of the same name — and there
*are* a Locust Street, a Columbia Street and a Union Avenue in Los Angeles.
They are kept for one reason: `lah-1899-10-11` states the **seniority rule**
for duplicate names, which Los Angeles adopts as annexation policy by 1910.

## Fourth pass — the Daily News and Evening Express, 1923–1935 (2026-09-06)

34 more clippings, now documents. **226 documents in the corpus.** Same finding as the
third pass, only sharper: **these papers never print a list either.** Not one of the 34
enumerates a multi-street ordinance. The largest, `lae-1930-05-03`, names five streets
because five is short enough to fit in a paragraph. For lists, the minutes remain the
only route.

But two of these clippings **closed questions the minutes probably could not have** —
`dnla-1927-06-30` and `lae-1927-10-18`, which show the Main Street ordinance was
ordered, drafted, and then withheld. A minute book showing a 10–4 vote and no
subsequent ordinance is silence; the newspaper says what the silence means.
