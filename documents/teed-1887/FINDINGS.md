## 1. ⭐⭐⭐ Ordinance No. 286 has no extent clause. The whole of Charity Street became Grand Avenue.

`handbook/COMPILED-ORDINANCES.md` ends on this and says only the text decides:

> Two readings, and only Ord. 286's text decides: (1) Ord. 286 renamed the
> **whole** length, and Violé's Charity is a different, later street in that
> southern cluster; or (2) Ord. 286 renamed only the **downtown stretch** …
> ⚠ On 2026-09-18 I told Kenny flatly that these were two different streets.
> That was premature … **Pull p. 248 and read the extent clause.**

Page 248 was pulled. **There is no extent clause.** The operative text is four
lines long:

> **Section 1.** That the name of that certain street in the City of Los
> Angeles, heretofore known as Charity street, be changed, and that hereupon and
> hereafter the same be known and designated as Grand avenue.

No from, no to, no portion, no tract. It quantifies over the *name*. That is
reading (1), and it is the unqualified case of
`handbook/change-rows-amendment.md` §2.

Adopted **February 15, 1887**, approved by Mayor pro tem **L. N. Breed** on the
21st, published in the **Los Angeles Daily Tribune** on the 22nd, which is when
it took effect. Full text and provenance: `documents/ord-286/`.

**A row is written** — `charity → grand-ave`, `scope: "whole-name"` — which is
the row `grand-ave`'s own internalNote says is "warranted but NOT written here:
no document in this corpus attests the 1886–87 act … That instrument is worth
finding (WANTED)." Found. Its extent derives from the eight documents that
letter `charity`.

### How far the name reached, from this volume's own pages

| source | says |
|---|---|
| Ord. 156 (p. 63) and Ord. 188 (p. 103) | lines of Charity street **from 84 ft north of Pico to Jefferson street**, a described strip **7,590 feet** long, from Feldhauser's subdivision of block 86 of Ord's survey to block 2 of Hancock's 35-acre lots |
| Ord. 156 §2 | assessment on both sides of Charity street **from Twelfth street to Jefferson street** |
| Res. 436 (p. 525) | Court House street, grade changed **from Charity street to Flower street** |
| Res. 387 (p. 476) | Charity street, **Seventh street to Ninth street** |
| Res. 391 (p. 479) | Fifth street, **from Charity street to Hope** |
| Res. 423 a (p. 513) | Pico street from Main street — indexed as "Charity street, grade of at Pico street" |
| Res. 488 (p. 579) | Washington street, Main to Figueroa — "**At the intersection of Grand avenue (Charity street)**" |
| Res. 432, 443 | limits of improvement for the **extension of Charity street** |

So the name ran continuously from Court House Street (between First and Temple)
south through Fifth, Seventh, Ninth, Pico and Washington to Jefferson. That is
the whole of modern Grand Avenue downtown.

### ⚠ What this does NOT settle

It does not prove that the CHARITY ST lettered in the virtue cluster on the
Violé map of 1904 is a different lineage. It proves only that **this** ordinance
left no part of **this** street behind. If a Charity Street was standing near
Jefferson in 1904, then either it is a separate entity that spells alike, or the
name was revived, or the ordinance was not enforced at the southern end. **This
is Kenny's call**, and it is now a much narrower one than it was.

⭐ **A lead for it:** **Ordinance No. 306** (p. 280, April 25, 1887) defines the
lines of Grand avenue by reference to a map filed with the Clerk **April 18,
1887**, entitled *"a plat showing extension and opening of Charity street."*
That plat would give Grand Avenue's exact 1887 extent, and two months after the
renaming the city was still titling it with the old name. **It is not in this
corpus. Worth asking the City Archivist for.** (WANTED.)

## 2. ⚠⚠ A date that does not fit: "Grand avenue (Charity street)" two weeks before the ordinance

Resolution No. 488, printed p. 579, establishing the grade of Washington street
from Main street to Figueroa street:

> At the intersection of Main street, the grade shall be 39.00 feet below the
> datum plane. **At the intersection of Grand avenue (Charity street)**, the
> grade shall be 42.00 feet below the datum plane. At the intersection of Hope
> street …

> *I hereby certify that the foregoing resolution was adopted by the Council of
> the City of Los Angeles, at its meeting of **February 1, 1887**.*

Ordinance No. 286 was adopted **February 15** and took effect **February 22**.
So the council wrote the new name, glossed with the old, a fortnight before it
enacted it.

**Three readings, and I cannot choose between them:**

1. **The name was already in use.** `charity`'s own note has the council voting
   on the change on **May 10, 1886** — nine months earlier — and the ordinance
   being the formality. The clerk drafting Res. 488 wrote the name everyone was
   already using and glossed it for the record. This is the same pattern as
   Winston Street (§7).
2. **Teed modernised it in 1887.** He compiled and set this volume after the
   change; a compositor's gloss is cheap.
3. **A misdated certification.** Less likely — Res. 487 and 489 both certify
   February 1, 1887 too.

Nothing in the volume decides it. Recorded on `documents/ord-286/` and here.

⚠ **Either way it is good evidence for the extent**, because it puts the Grand
Avenue name at **Washington Street**, which is well south of Pico. That is
independent of which reading is right.

## 3. ⭐⭐⭐ Lemon Street, alive and placed in 1884 — and no Wilson Street anywhere

The brief asked me to flag anything bearing on the 1903 street index's finding
that **Lemon and Wilson are two separate streets both present in 1903**, which
contradicts `lemon-st-arts-district`'s reading that Lemon *became* Wilson in
1887. This volume has two things to say and they both point the same way.

### a. Resolution No. 240 puts Lemon Street on the corridor, in March 1884

Printed p. 315, establishing the grade of Seventh street from San Pedro street
to the Los Angeles river. Its grade points, in order:

> At the intersection of Seventh street and San Pedro street … At a point …
> 1800 feet easterly from the east boundary of San Pedro street … At a point …
> 2100 feet easterly … **At the intersection of Alameda street and Seventh
> street** … **At the intersection of Lemon street and Seventh street**, the
> grade shall be 17 feet below the datum plane. At a point in the center line of
> Seventh street **368 feet easterly from the east boundary of Lemon street** …
> And at a point … **1687 feet easterly from the east boundary of Lemon
> street** …
> *adopted … at its meeting of March 10, 1884.*

**Lemon Street is the first cross street east of Alameda on Seventh Street in
March 1884**, and the resolution measures 368 ft and 1,687 ft further east from
it. That is the Arts District corridor, three years earlier than the two 1887
sheets (`mr013-091`, `mr018-025`) the entity currently rests on.

### b. There is no Wilson Street in the GENERAL INDEX

The index's W run, printed p. 682, goes:

> Wall street … Walnut street … Walters street … Warren street … Washington
> street … Water pipes … Water street … Well street … Whittier street …
> **Wilhart street** … **William street** … **Willow street** … **Wilmington
> street** … **Winston street** … Wood avenue … Workman … Workman street …

**Wilhart, William, Willow, Wilmington, Winston. No Wilson.** The same gap
Caswell 1878 and Robinson 1884 show, per `handbook/COMPILED-ORDINANCES.md`.

### What I think this adds up to — and it is not what the project has assumed

⚠ **Absence from a subject index is weak evidence**, as COMPILED-ORDINANCES.md
already says: a street with no council business simply does not appear. But the
positive half is not weak. The city graded Seventh Street *through* Lemon Street
in 1884 and measured two more points east of it. Lemon was a working, graded,
named cross street of Seventh east of Alameda, and it stayed one: the sibling
volume found Ord. 428 of November 1888 defining Ninth street's lines "west of
Lemon street".

Set beside the 1903 index showing **Lemon and Wilson as two separate streets
both present**, the reading that Lemon *became* Wilson in 1887 is now carrying
much more weight than its evidence. `lemon-st-arts-district`'s internalNote is
already honest about this — *"⚠ KINES READS IT OTHERWISE, calling Lemon a
'nearby' street … DO NOT write a Lemon → Wilson change row until the modern
Lemon Street's line has been checked against this corridor"* — and nothing here
changes that instruction. But the balance has shifted.

**Recommendation (Kenny's call):** `lemon-st-arts-district`'s `note` currently
says the question is "unsettled". That is still true, but the note could say
which way the evidence now leans, and cite Res. 240 as the earliest dated
sighting of the name. The cheap next step is unchanged and now overdue: **widen
the OSM extract one block south and check modern Lemon Street's line against
this corridor.**

## 6. ⭐⭐ The city says Kohler Street became EIGHTH. Kines says Ninth.

`kohler`'s internalNote records Kines's account:

> "⚠ TWO KOHLER STREETS. Kines says the ORIGINAL Kohler Street ran where **9th
> Street** now runs between San Pedro and Alameda, and that the current one was
> named in 1887."

**Ordinance No. 187, adopted June 30, 1885**, is titled *"An Ordinance changing
the name of Kohler street to **Eighth** street"*, and its Section 1, the LIST OF
ORDINANCES and the GENERAL INDEX all say Eighth.

They cannot both be right, and the city's own printed ordinance is the better
witness. **Recommendation (Kenny's call):** `kohler`'s internalNote should record
that the primary record contradicts Kines on the successor name, and date the
first Kohler Street's disappearance to **August 30, 1885** (its publication, and
thus its effective date).

⚠ Note the knock-on: if the old Kohler became Eighth Street rather than Ninth,
then Kines's "between San Pedro and Alameda" is the stretch of **modern 8th
Street** in the Arts District — which is where `lemon-st-arts-district` and its
neighbours are. Worth keeping in view when §3 is settled.

⚠ Also in the same index entry: *"Kohler street, name of changed to Eighth
street … 187 … 187"* — the PAGE column repeats the ordinance number. The page is
102.

## 7. ⭐⭐ Names in use before, and after, the ordinances that make them — four cases in one volume

This volume shows the same pattern four times, and the sibling volume found a
fifth. It is worth stating as a pattern, because it means **an ordinance date is
a bound on the name's officialness, not on its use.**

| name | ordinance | the city using the name outside that window |
|---|---|---|
| **Winston street** | Ord. 320 creates it **July 5, 1887** (`documents/ord-320`, in Teed 1889) | **Res. 478, adopted January 3, 1887** — grade of Wall street, Mayo to Fifth, intersections Mayo, Boyd, Fourth, **Winston street**, Fifth. *Six months early*, and on the very block Ordinance No. 48 of 1889 would later call Shaw street. The sibling volume found Winston on Mills' Tract in 1886 as well. Three sightings now. |
| **Grand avenue** | Ord. 286, **February 15, 1887** | **Res. 488, adopted February 1, 1887** — "at the intersection of **Grand avenue (Charity street)**". *Two weeks early.* See §2. |
| **Guadalupe street** | Ord. 242 renames it Davies, **July 19, 1886** | **Res. 508, adopted March 7, 1887** — "the grade of **Guadalupe street**, from Alameda street to Garey street". *Eight months late.* |
| **Short street** | Ord. 152 folds it into Bellevue avenue, **January 13, 1885** | **Res. 313** — "defining the limits of **Short street** from Buena Vista street westerly to Canal and Reservoir street **and dedicating the same to public uses as a street**". Numbered well after the ordinance. *Later.* |

⚠ **Consequence for the model, and it is not a small one.** A `change` row pins a
transition at the ordinance's date. These four cases say the ordinance is the
legal moment, not the moment the name arrived or left the page. That is the
right thing for the data to record — a dated instrument is exactly what the model
wants — but anyone reading a map segment's date off a change row should know the
ground was fuzzier than the row. Worth a sentence in
`handbook/RENAMING-SOURCES.md` if Kenny agrees.

## 9. ⚠⚠ Series, and the folder-name collision risk

**Ordinances Nos. 114–317 in this volume are OLD SERIES numbers.** Before
November 1883 the city did not number its ordinances at all — the numbers in
Robinson 1884 are the compiler's, ratified retrospectively — and this volume
continues that run. The **1889 charter restarted the numbering at 1 (New
Series)**, so `ord-<n>` is ambiguous across the two series.

**Checked: there are no collisions in the corpus today.** The `documents/ord-*`
folders now hold

```
OLD SERIES:  76 77 81 88 92 97 103 105        (Robinson 1884)
             152 187 200 207 242 257 275 286 314 317   (this volume, new today)
             320 345 381                      (Teed 1889)
NEW SERIES:  1613 4093 162010 169111 171586 172590
DATED:       1874-02-26  1887-08-22
```

Old-series numbers run 76–432; the New Series folders in the corpus are all
four digits or more. **No folder name is ambiguous.**

⚠ **But the risk is live and it is not hypothetical.** Every folder made from
this volume says **"(Old Series)"** in its `title`, as does `documents/ord-286`'s
header. Two specific hazards to watch:

1. **New Series No. 48** — the April 1889 duplicate-name cull — is in the corpus
   as `documents/lah-1889-05-10`, not `ord-48`. If it is ever re-filed under a
   number, it must not be `ord-48` without a series marker, because Robinson
   1884's old-series 48 is a real ordinance too.
2. **New Series Nos. 152, 187, 200, 207, 242, 257, 275, 286, 314 and 317 all
   exist**, somewhere in the 1889–90 run, and any of them that turns out to
   matter will collide head-on with a folder created today. The convention that
   would fix it — `ord-ns-<n>` for the New Series, or `ord-<n>-os` — is Kenny's
   to set. **I have not invented one**, because a naming convention adopted by
   one agent in one batch is worse than none.
